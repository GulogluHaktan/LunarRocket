This subsection defines the learning problem on which the three blending methods are compared in real simulation, and the reinforcement learning (RL) method used to solve that problem. The algorithm and hyperparameter configuration were never changed among the three methods (see Table 1); this subsection therefore describes the "fixed" component of the comparison.

Formulation of the task as a Markov decision process (MDP). The landing task is treated as a discrete-time MDP (S, A, P, r, gamma). The state observation is 125-dimensional and consists of the following component groups: position relative to the target, the body's linear and angular velocity in the world frame, the orientation quaternion, the instantaneous thrust acceleration transformed into the body frame, the minimum foot-to-ground clearance, the current and previous action vectors, local terrain slope/roughness metrics around the target, a 64-ray LiDAR profile scanning the surroundings of the body, a 24-point local terrain height scan below the target, and (noise-added) sensor features derived from IMU/altimeter readings. All components are scaled to the [-1, 1] or [0, 1] band by their physical ranges and clipped against outliers with clip_obs = 10. The action space is 3-dimensional and continuous, [-1, 1]^3: the first component is the main thrust command and is effectively mapped to the 0-0.35 throttle range (roughly 10.5 N); the second and third components are the two-axis gimbal (thrust-vectoring) angle, and the envelope the policy can command scales with curriculum difficulty between +-4 deg and +-8 deg (the mechanical hard stop is at +-25 deg). The transition dynamics P are the physics simulation solved on NVIDIA PhysX at 120 Hz; the policy produces actions at 60 Hz with decimation = 2, and each episode lasts 20 seconds, i.e. 1200 control steps. The discount factor is gamma = 0.99.

Reward function. The reward is the sum of dense and terminal terms: r = xy_progress + altitude_progress + stability + velocity + guidance + control + terrain + time + terminal. Each dense term is defined either as a progress difference between two consecutive steps or as a per-second cost; this design prevents an early crash from becoming attractive merely because it stops the step penalties. The largest weights concentrate on horizontal approach to the target (+12.0 per unit decrease in distance), deviation from the desired vertical descent speed (-8.0), and guidance deviation from the desired thrust-acceleration profile (-6.0); the remaining dense terms penalize tilt relative to the local terrain normal, angular velocity, fuel/gimbal usage, action smoothness, and the fit of the footprint to the terrain. The terminal term is paid only once per episode and depends on the landing classification: soft landing +300, harsh landing -220 (with a correction between 0 and +160 depending on landing quality), out-of-bounds/crash -220, timeout -150. The classification is made according to foot contact, the vehicle's residual tilt relative to the local terrain normal (not world-vertical), and speed/footprint-fit thresholds. The detailed rationale for the reward weights is documented in the source comments of the production code; it was not changed during this study's comparison.

Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). The policy was learned with SAC, an off-policy actor-critic algorithm with a maximum-entropy objective (Haarnoja et al., 2018). SAC optimizes not only the expected reward but an objective regularized by the policy's entropy: pi* = argmax_pi sum_t E[r(s_t, a_t) + alpha H(pi(. | s_t))]. The entropy term H here encourages the agent to remain stochastic even as it converges toward high reward; in a contact-rich landing problem with many local optima, this delays the policy prematurely turning deterministic and locking into a weak behaviour (premature convergence). SAC's sample efficiency -- obtained by reusing a replay buffer repeatedly -- and its relative robustness to hyperparameters are the reasons this algorithm was chosen for a physics simulation that is expensive even when GPU-parallel. In practice, two independent Q-critic networks (clipped double-Q, which reduces value overestimation bias), target networks updated slowly by Polyak averaging (tau = 0.005), and automatic tuning of the temperature coefficient alpha by anchoring it to a target entropy are used; alpha is learned during training by the gradient of the objective J(alpha) = E[-alpha (log pi(a | s) + H_target)]. Concretely, the SAC implementation of the Stable-Baselines3 library was used (Raffin et al., 2021).

Network architecture and hyperparameters. The actor and critic networks are multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with hidden layer sizes [384, 256, 128] and ELU activation. The replay buffer has a capacity of 10^6 transitions; the first 16,384 transitions are for buffer filling only (learning_starts). The batch size is 1024, the learning rate is 3 x 10^-4 (Adam), gamma = 0.99, tau = 0.005. Each vectorized environment step produces 512 transitions, and 32 gradient steps are taken per vector step (train_freq = 1, gradient_steps = 32): a single update per vector step markedly under-trains SAC, whereas 32 updates provide enough policy updates for the curriculum while preserving parallel-simulation throughput. Observations and value targets are normalized with running statistics (normalize_input and normalize_value on). This normalization solves a scale problem: because terminal rewards are on the order of +300/-220 while dense terms can accumulate to over +-1000 per episode, without normalization the critic/value targets dominate the SAC update scale, and the temperature coefficient collapses from 0.85 to 0.025 in about 50 thousand steps, turning the actor deterministic before it has learned anything meaningful. For the same reason the target entropy is set to -1.5 instead of the default -action_dim = -3; this value leaves markedly more exploration in the early curriculum phases. Curriculum. Training is carried out within a curriculum in which the spawn distance, the target distance, and the gimbal authority the policy can command increase together. The difficulty level is a scalar in [0.05, 1.0]; for each episode the effective difficulty is sampled as torch.rand() x level, i.e. even at the highest level the episodes in a batch are distributed from easy to hard. If the rolling-window success rate exceeds the threshold curriculum_success_threshold = 0.35, the level increases by 0.05; if it falls below half the threshold, it decreases by 0.05. The window size scales with the number of environments (at least 4 x num_envs completed episodes): a fixed, small window fills with only a few resets at high environment counts, causing difficulty to race toward ~1.0 much faster than the policy has actually learned, and then a measurable performance regression (a training collapse documented in the README). Similarly, since the gimbal envelope saturating in the first 30% of the curriculum produced a reproducible collapse when the hardest episodes of a batch suddenly hit the harsh 8 deg gimbal saturation, this envelope is spread across the entire curriculum range (curriculum_full_gimbal_difficulty = 1.0). These two arrangements are common to all of the comparison runs described below in Section 3.6.
