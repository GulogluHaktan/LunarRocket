The findings give, first of all, a clear answer to RQ1: the seam left by the as-shipped Gaussian blend when truncated at a finite radius is not a cosmetic or negligible defect but a measurable and statistically extremely significant structural defect, roughly three orders of magnitude worse than smoothstep (about 3200x on C0, 530x on C1). The source of this defect is not a coding error but a property of radial basis functions known for more than fifty years: the Gaussian-type radial functions defined by Hardy (1971) are infinite-support and never reach exactly zero at any finite radius; Wendland (1995) proposed compact-support, minimal-degree positive-definite radial functions precisely as the solution to this problem. The contribution of this study is to demonstrate this fifty-year-old mathematical knowledge quantitatively in a concrete engineering setting (a physically queryable lunar-surface height field) and to test whether it is reflected in an RL training run. This analytic finding, unlike the RL results discussed below, is validated with 30 seeds and is not subject to single-run uncertainty; it is therefore the study's most robust, least contentious finding.

In answer to RQ2, the proposed hybrid method preserves the Gaussian's soft "bell" profile in the contact region and smoothstep's compact support beyond it, while creating no new seam at its own internal transition (switch); this is an expected result consistent with the partition-of-unity framework described by Ohtake et al. (2003): the convex combination of two C1-smooth functions with a C1-smooth weight is again C1-smooth. The price of this advantage is a microsecond cost roughly 4.3 times that of smoothstep in computing the pure fade formula (because both branches are computed). This formula-level cost is reflected only partially and inconsistently in the real training wall-clock time: in the first (seed=42) measurement the Gaussian and hybrid were 56% and 35% slower respectively, but averaged over the three seeds this difference drops to 20% and 12.5%, and the standard deviation (20-30% of the mean) shows how much a single run can deviate (Section 4.3). This suggests that the real wall-clock difference stems largely from run-to-run system variability (background load, thermal state, terrain-pool timing), with the pure formula cost contributing only a small and consistent amount; this is a separate observation, one that should not be over-interpreted but that shows single-run measurements can also be misleading in cost comparisons.

The answer to RQ3 changed substantially after multiple seeds, and that change is itself one of the study's most instructive findings. In the first (single-seed, seed=42) measurement, the Gaussian and hybrid appeared to reach a final success rate roughly 8-12 percentage points higher than smoothstep, and in the first draft this was attributed to a mechanism such as a "difference in the transition-band weight profile." In the measurement extended with two additional independent seeds (seed=7, seed=123) (Section 4.3), the difference among methods shrank (the Gaussian-smoothstep difference from 11% to 4.7%), the hybrid-smoothstep difference changed sign, and no pairwise comparison came out statistically significant on any of the three metrics (success rate, timeout rate, harsh-landing rate) (all p>=0.20). Recalling that the analytic boundary continuity of the Gaussian variant used in the real training (calibrated, k=ln(1000)) is already almost equal to smoothstep (Table 2), this result actually shows an expected consistency: if there is no measurable difference in boundary continuity, it is no surprise that there is no strong difference in RL training performance either. The apparent difference in the first measurement was most likely ordinary run-to-run variance stemming from the inherent nondeterminism of the GPU-parallel PhysX simulation and of the terrain pool's background CPU generation process -- exactly the pseudoreplication pitfall Henderson et al. (2018) warn about. Because this study refuted its own initial finding in this way, it provides an in-house case study of how misleading single-run RL comparisons can be: going from n=1 to n=3 was enough to turn a seemingly "significant" finding into a statistically indistinguishable one. That said, n=3 is still low-powered, and the current data cannot rule out the existence of a small real effect either (in particular, the Gaussian consistently having the highest mean); reaching a firm conclusion requires more seeds (Section 6).

The scope of this study was deliberately kept narrow so that the effect of the blending function could be isolated as a single variable; the three methods were compared only with each other, holding the rest of the production environment (physics engine, reward function, agent architecture, curriculum) fixed. Porting another terrain-blending method from the literature -- e.g. one implemented in another simulator -- directly into this environment as an external benchmark was deliberately not chosen; such a benchmark would make it impossible to separate the effect of the blending function from the countless other differences in that simulator's physics engine, observation/reward design, and agent architecture. The cost of this is that the extent to which the findings generalize beyond this specific environment (Isaac Lab/PhysX, SAC, this task definition) -- the external validity -- is not tested in this study; this point is addressed as a concrete future-work direction in Section 6.

The most important limitation of this study is that it still has low statistical power with n=3: with the current data, the smallest p value the Mann-Whitney test can produce is already limited (around a minimum of p=0.10 in a 3-versus-3 comparison), so the "no significant difference" finding does not prove that the methods are genuinely equivalent -- it only shows that the large apparent difference in the first measurement could not be reliably confirmed. A second limitation is that the training scale (5.73 million steps, roughly 350 iterations) is below the full-scale configuration (1200 iterations) because of the compute budget; no run may have reached full convergence, which means the observed plateaus may reflect an interim performance difference rather than a final/asymptotic one. A third limitation is that the analytic/CPU study isolates only the fade formula itself and does not fully separate the source of the real training wall-clock difference (terrain generation, physics stepping, system variability) -- although the three-seed data shows this difference shrinking, it does not pin down its source with certainty.
