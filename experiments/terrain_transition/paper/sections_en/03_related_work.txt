This section reviews the literature in which the study is situated, under five thematic clusters. The review is a targeted (scoping) review; it is not a PRISMA-compliant systematic review, and within each cluster the most-cited/foundational references of the field are prioritized.

Cluster 1 concerns multi-resolution blending and level-of-detail (LOD) transitions in procedural terrain. Geometry clipmaps (Losasso & Hoppe, 2004), ROAM (Duchaineau et al., 1997), and geomipmapping (de Boer, 2000) form exactly the traditional computer-graphics lineage to which this study's two-mesh (global + local patch) framing belongs; all three treat the formation of cracks/seams at the boundary where a low- and a high-resolution mesh meet as a central engineering problem. This study's finding -- that the as-shipped Gaussian leaves a real seam when truncated at a finite radius -- is a concrete, measured instance of the same class of problem this literature has been trying to solve for more than twenty-five years; and smoothstep's compact support coincides with the principle, used by ROAM and geoclipmaps, of a weighting function that goes exactly to zero at the boundary.

Cluster 2 concerns the continuity properties of compact-support and infinite-support blending functions. The multiquadric/Gaussian-type radial basis function that Hardy (1971) proposed for topography is, like the family to which this study's as-shipped Gaussian belongs, infinite-support and never reaches exactly zero at any finite radius; this is consistent with the measured residual behaviour (approximately exp(-1) ~ 37%). Wendland (1995) formalizes the solution: compact-support, minimal-degree positive-definite radial functions for a given smoothness order; smoothstep (cubic Hermite, Ebert et al., 2002) is a one-dimensional, low-degree special case of this family. Ohtake et al. (2003) describe the strategy followed by this study's hybrid method: building a multi-scale hierarchy from compact-support basis functions to obtain different blending behaviour at different scales.

Cluster 3 concerns terrain modelling and contact dynamics in planetary/lunar landing simulations. The goal of the ALHAT program (Johnson & Montgomery, 2008; NASA JPL, n.d.) -- precise landing within one hundred metres and LiDAR-based terrain-relative navigation -- and the accuracy level offered by LOLA/SELENE-based digital elevation models (Barker et al., 2016) show that in real lunar-landing engineering, terrain accuracy is not cosmetic but a direct operational requirement. This strengthens the study's "why seams/continuity matter" motivation: a discontinuity in the foot contact region corresponds, in real ALHAT-class systems, to a class of error that can affect hazard-detection and landing-site-selection decisions. This motivation is also corroborated by recent flight data: the vision-based navigation and obstacle-detection results of JAXA's SLIM mission (January 2024; Ishida et al., 2025) showed that real-time obstacle/terrain detection performed 50 m before landing yielded metre-level landing precision -- concretely demonstrating that errors in terrain representation are reflected directly in landing safety not only in simulation but in real flight.

Cluster 4 concerns procedural terrain randomization in physics-based RL environments. Rudin et al. (2022) use, on IsaacGym, a difficulty-based terrain curriculum similar to the terrain pool in this study; this shows that the study's curriculum and terrain-pool design is not an isolated instance but an established practice in the field. Mittal et al. (2023, Orbit) and NVIDIA (2025, Isaac Lab) define the architectural basis of the very platform this study uses, while Tobin et al. (2017) provide the general motivation for procedural randomization in sim-to-real transfer. None of these sources, however, treats the multi-resolution blending function itself (smoothstep, Gaussian, or hybrid) as a design variable within terrain randomization; all focus on terrain diversity, not on the effect of the blend shape on continuity and learning cost. This study is positioned precisely in that gap.

Cluster 5 concerns hybrid/regional combination of blending functions (partition of unity). Ohtake et al. (2003) are again the central source here: the multi-scale, partition-of-unity-style combination of compact-support basis functions is the general mathematical framework of this study's hybrid method (one function in the contact region, another beyond it, with a smooth switch between them). Under this framework, the hybrid switch not creating a new seam at machine precision is an expected result consistent with the literature: the convex combination of two C1-smooth functions with a C1-smooth weight is again C1-smooth.

In summary, Cluster 1 supplies the problem class (seams/continuity in multi-resolution transitions) but this literature is largely focused on visual quality; this study measures the same problem numerically on a physically queryable height field (foot contact). Cluster 2 grounds why the empirical finding is correct in a mathematical result more than fifty years old; this is not a coding error but a known structural property of radial basis functions. Cluster 3 provides real-world engineering justification for the "why does it matter" question. Cluster 4 clarifies where this study's experimental platform sits in the literature and the gap it fills. Cluster 5 moves the proposed hybrid method from being an isolated engineering trick to sitting within an established mathematical framework.
