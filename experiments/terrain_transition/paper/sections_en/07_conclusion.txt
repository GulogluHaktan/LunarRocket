This study systematically examined the shape of the blending function used at the boundary where a coarse global terrain and a fine-detail local patch meet, in an Isaac Lab-based lunar landing environment, both at the analytic/CPU level and in a real Isaac Sim SAC training run. Analytic measurements over thirty seeds showed, with statistical significance, that the previously used as-shipped Gaussian blend produces boundary continuity three orders of magnitude worse than smoothstep when truncated at a finite radius, and that this defect is a scale-invariant, structural property. The proposed hybrid method and a calibrated Gaussian variant both removed this defect to near machine precision -- this is the study's most robust and least contentious finding.

The real Isaac Sim training comparison began with a single seed per method, and in the first measurement showed the Gaussian and hybrid reaching a markedly higher success rate than smoothstep. To test the reliability of this finding, the study was extended with two additional independent seeds (n=3 in total), and no statistically significant difference was found among the methods on any metric. This is the study's second important contribution: it provides a concrete, self-sourced example of how misleading single-run RL comparisons can be, and it empirically confirms the warning of Henderson et al. (2018) in this specific environment. Considered together with the analytic results, this shows that the choice of blending function has a measurable and robust effect on boundary continuity, but that this is not reflected -- at least at this scale and with n=3, in a distinguishable way -- in final RL training performance.

The study's core contribution is a methodology that tests, quantitatively, whether the choice of blending function is merely a visual/rendering preference, in a real Isaac Sim/Isaac Lab environment at both the analytic and the RL-training level; this methodology was able to report a "no significant difference" result as reliably as a positive one. All three methods are integrated into the production code as a backward-compatible, selectable configuration, so these findings are directly actionable: with the current data, there is no statistical basis for choosing among the three methods in terms of RL training performance; the choice can be based on other criteria such as boundary continuity (analytically in favour of the Gaussian and hybrid) or computational cost (in favour of smoothstep).

Three concrete directions are proposed for future work. First, because n=3 still has low statistical power, the real training comparison should be extended with at least five to ten independent seeds, to determine conclusively whether the small mean differences observed (in particular, the Gaussian consistently having the highest mean) are a real effect or residual noise. Second, the training scale should be raised to the full configuration (1200 iterations), to confirm whether the observed plateaus reflect a final/asymptotic performance difference rather than an interim one. Third, going beyond the deliberately narrow scope of this study (see Section 5), the external validity of the findings should be tested: repeating the same three blending methods on another physics engine, another reward/observation design, or another contact-rich task (e.g. quadruped locomotion, humanoid standing) would separate whether the findings here depend on the blending function or on the other design choices of this specific environment.
